# Non-Shooting Guard Archetype

## Purpose

This document defines the non-shooting guard archetype and explains why this type of player can be difficult to fit on certain NBA rosters.

Use this document when evaluating players who create value through athleticism, rim pressure, passing, or transition play but do not reliably space the floor.

## Definition

A non-shooting guard is a guard whose primary value comes from skills other than outside shooting.

Common strengths:

- Rim pressure
- Transition offense
- Ball-handling
- Passing
- Rebounding
- Athleticism
- Pace
- Paint touches
- Defensive pressure, depending on the player

Common weaknesses:

- Poor three-point gravity
- Limited off-ball value
- Crowded spacing
- Reduced playoff scalability
- Easier defensive game-planning
- Awkward fit next to other ball-dominant players

## How This Archetype Creates Value

A non-shooting guard can be valuable when surrounded by:

- Strong shooting
- Vertical spacing
- Defensive bigs
- Off-ball wings
- Low-usage finishers
- Lineups that allow the guard to control pace

This archetype can raise a team’s regular-season floor because the player can create shots, push tempo, and pressure the rim.

## Where the Archetype Becomes Risky

This archetype becomes risky when paired with:

- Another ball-dominant star
- Limited shooting
- Paint-oriented bigs
- Half-court playoff settings
- Teams that already have enough creation
- Stars who need spacing to maximize their value

The problem is not only missed shots. The larger issue is how defenses can ignore or help off a weak shooter, shrinking the floor for everyone else.

## Playoff Concerns

In the playoffs, opponents can game-plan more aggressively.

Common defensive tactics against non-shooting guards:

- Go under screens
- Help off the guard when he is off-ball
- Pack the paint
- Force jump shots
- Shrink driving lanes
- Make the player decide quickly in traffic

If the guard cannot punish these coverages, the team’s best lineups may become less efficient.

## Fit Next to Ball-Dominant Stars

A non-shooting guard next to a ball-dominant star creates a specific issue:

- The guard needs the ball to be maximized.
- The star also needs the ball to create elite offense.
- If the guard plays off-ball, defenders may ignore him.
- If the guard plays on-ball, the star may be used less efficiently.

This creates role tension.

## Good Fit Conditions

A non-shooting guard can still be a good fit if:

- The team has elite shooting at multiple other positions.
- The guard is a high-level defender.
- The guard accepts a lower-usage role.
- The team can stagger minutes effectively.
- The guard is used in transition-heavy lineups.
- The team has a stretch big or five-out spacing.

## Bad Fit Conditions

A non-shooting guard is a poor fit if:

- The team’s stars need driving lanes.
- The team lacks shooting.
- The guard is not a strong defender.
- The guard has a large contract.
- The team already has a primary creator.
- The guard cannot close playoff games reliably.

## Trade Evaluation Relevance

When a team trades for a non-shooting guard, evaluate:

- Is the team paying star-level salary for a player with fit limitations?
- Does the player solve a real weakness?
- Does the player create a new spacing problem?
- Can the team afford to build around the archetype?
- Is the player’s role clear?
- Can he play in the team’s best playoff lineups?

## Reusable Trade Evaluation Sentence

“The player adds creation and pressure, but his limited shooting reduces lineup flexibility and creates spacing concerns, especially next to stars who already need the ball.”
