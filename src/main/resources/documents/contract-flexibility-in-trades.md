# Contract Flexibility in NBA Trades

## Purpose

This document explains how contracts affect trade evaluation beyond the player’s on-court talent.

Use this document when evaluating whether a trade helped or hurt a team’s future flexibility.

## Core Idea

A player’s contract is part of the asset.

A good player on a bad contract can be a risky trade target. A role player on a flexible contract can be more useful than his raw box-score numbers suggest.

## Contract Factors to Evaluate

Important contract factors include:

- Annual salary
- Contract length
- Age curve
- Player option
- Team option
- Injury risk
- Trade value
- Salary matching value
- Luxury tax pressure
- Apron restrictions
- Extension eligibility
- Positional scarcity
- Expected market value

## Positive Contract

A positive contract gives the team surplus value.

A contract is usually positive when:

- The player produces more than his salary suggests.
- The player fits many roster types.
- The contract is movable.
- The salary helps with trade matching.
- The player is young or in his prime.
- The contract does not block future moves.

## Neutral Contract

A neutral contract is roughly fair market value.

A neutral contract does not add major surplus value, but it also does not meaningfully damage flexibility.

## Negative Contract

A negative contract is a contract where the salary is meaningfully higher than the player’s expected on-court value or trade market value.

Negative contracts matter because they can:

- Reduce flexibility
- Require draft assets to move
- Limit roster upgrades
- Increase luxury tax pressure
- Reduce the number of realistic trade partners
- Force a team to accept worse trade outcomes later

## Large Salary Is Not Always Bad

A large salary can be useful if:

- The player is a true star.
- The player fits the team’s core.
- The player is still tradable.
- The salary can be used for matching in a future trade.
- The contract expires soon and creates flexibility.

Large salary becomes problematic when the player is a poor fit, declining, hard to trade, or not worth the role he occupies.

## Opportunity Cost

Contract evaluation should include opportunity cost.

Ask:

- What else could the team have done with this salary?
- Could the outgoing contracts have been used in a different trade?
- Did the team lose multiple flexible contracts for one inflexible contract?
- Did the team spend picks to acquire or later move the contract?
- Does the contract limit midseason corrections?

## Contender Context

For contenders, contract flexibility matters because the team may need to adjust around its stars.

A contender should be careful about trades that:

- Remove multiple playoff-caliber role players
- Add a difficult contract
- Reduce optionality
- Make the team dependent on minimum contracts
- Force future asset spending just to undo the move

## Rebuilding Team Context

For rebuilding teams, large contracts can be acceptable if the team receives enough draft compensation or young talent.

A bad contract is not always bad for a rebuilding team if it is used to buy assets.

## Trade Evaluation Questions

When evaluating a trade’s cap or contract impact, ask:

- Did the team improve its salary structure?
- Did the team gain or lose flexibility?
- Is the incoming contract movable?
- Did the team take on salary risk?
- Did the team increase future tax pressure?
- Did the team lose useful matching salary?
- Did the team make future trades easier or harder?

## Reusable Trade Evaluation Sentence

“The contract impact is negative because the team consolidated flexible salary and useful depth into a larger, harder-to-move contract, reducing its ability to rebalance the roster later.”
